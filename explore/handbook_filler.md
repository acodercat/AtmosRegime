# Reference notes: general rules of thumb

## General

### Rest a roast before carving (about one minute per 100 g, up to 20 minutes)
Valid when: the joint is a medium or large cut of meat, such as a leg of lamb, a whole chicken or a beef rib, it will be sliced across the grain for serving at the table, and it can be kept loosely covered with foil in a warm kitchen while the gravy and vegetables are finished.
Outside that: thin steaks, chops and sausages need only two to five minutes on a warm plate; fish fillets and stir-fried strips should be served at once, because resting them only lets them go cold and dry. A very large turkey can rest for 30 to 40 minutes under foil and a clean tea towel without losing much warmth.

### Bake a standard sponge at 180 °C for 20 to 25 minutes in two 20 cm tins
Valid when: the recipe uses equal weights of butter, sugar, flour and eggs, the tins are greased and lined with baking paper, the oven has been switched on well ahead of time, and it is a conventional oven with the shelf placed in the middle.
Outside that: in a fan oven lower the setting by about 20 °C; a single deep tin needs 40 to 50 minutes at a lower setting, and cupcakes need about 18 minutes. Test with a skewer, which should come out clean, rather than trusting the clock, and avoid opening the door during the first 15 minutes, or the middle may sink.

### Salt pasta water generously, about one tablespoon per four litres
Valid when: the pasta is dried durum-wheat pasta served with a sauce that is not itself very salty, the pan is large enough for the pasta to move freely, and the water is drained off at the end rather than absorbed into the dish.
Outside that: with a salty sauce such as one built on anchovies, capers, olives or cured pork, halve the salt; for risotto or for pasta cooked in its own sauce, season the stock and the dish gradually and taste as you go. Keep a cupful of the cooking water back before draining, to loosen the sauce if it becomes too thick.

### Use cold butter and minimal handling for shortcrust pastry
Valid when: the pastry is a plain shortcrust for pies or tarts, the butter goes in straight from the fridge in small cubes, the mixture is rubbed in by hand or pulsed briefly in a processor, and it is then chilled for at least 30 minutes before rolling.
Outside that: choux pastry is made with melted butter on the hob and beaten vigorously; enriched bread doughs such as brioche need long kneading to develop gluten, so the rule of light handling applies to tender pastry only. If shortcrust cracks badly when rolled, let it stand for a few minutes rather than adding more water.

### Knead bread dough for about ten minutes, until it passes the windowpane test
Valid when: the dough is a lean, yeasted wheat dough with roughly 60 to 70 percent water to flour by weight, made with strong bread flour, kneaded by hand on a lightly floured worktop, and left to rise afterwards in a covered bowl.
Outside that: very wet doughs, such as ciabatta, are better developed with a series of stretch-and-fold sessions during a long rise; rye doughs never form a windowpane, so judge them by feel and by the rise instead. In a stand mixer with a dough hook, six to eight minutes on a low speed is usually enough.

### Water a vegetable garden deeply once or twice a week, not lightly every day
Valid when: the plants are established vegetables or shrubs in open ground with reasonable soil, the roots have had a few weeks to spread, and the weather is ordinary for the season rather than a long drought or a very wet spell.
Outside that: seedlings, new transplants and plants in containers need daily checks and often daily watering; in a long dry spell water early in the morning and mulch the beds, and in heavy clay soil water less often but more slowly. Aim the can or hose at the base of each plant rather than over the leaves.

### Sow seeds at a depth of about twice their own width
Valid when: the seeds are medium-sized, such as beans, peas, squash or sweetcorn, they are sown directly into prepared, crumbly ground or into a tray of seed compost, and the packet gives no specific depth of its own.
Outside that: very fine seeds, such as lettuce, foxgloves and many herbs, are pressed onto the surface and barely covered, since many need to see daylight to sprout; large bulbs follow their own guide of about three times their height. Where the seed packet gives a depth, follow the packet instead of this rule.

### Prune spring-flowering shrubs straight after they finish flowering
Valid when: the shrub, such as forsythia, lilac, weigela or flowering currant, produces its blooms on wood that grew during the previous year, it has become crowded or untidy, and the aim is to keep it shapely and flowering well.
Outside that: shrubs that flower on the current year's growth, such as buddleja and many hardy fuchsias, are cut back hard in early spring; leave evergreens and many fruit trees to their own pruning calendars, and remove dead wood at any time. Use clean, sharp secateurs and cut just above a healthy bud.

### Rotate vegetable crops through four beds over four years
Valid when: the garden has room for separate beds for legumes, brassicas, roots and potatoes, the same layout will be kept for several seasons, and pests or diseases that build up in the soil, such as clubroot, have been a problem in the past.
Outside that: in a small plot, simply avoid growing the same family in the same spot two years running; perennial crops such as asparagus, rhubarb and soft fruit stay put and are kept out of the rotation altogether. A simple notebook sketch of each year's beds makes the plan much easier to follow.

### Harden off seedlings for one to two weeks before planting out
Valid when: the plants were raised indoors, on a windowsill or under glass, they have several true leaves, and they will be moved to an open bed outdoors once the last spring frost has passed in your area.
Outside that: seeds sown directly outdoors need no hardening off; plants bought from an outdoor nursery bench are usually already acclimatised, and for those raised under cover start with an hour or two outside on a mild, sheltered day. Bring the trays back in at night for the first few days.

### Set body text at 10 to 12 points with leading of about 120 to 145 percent
Valid when: the text is continuous prose in a book, report or letter, set in a conventional serif or sans-serif face, printed on paper at normal reading distance, and read by adults without particular needs for larger print.
Outside that: captions and footnotes go down to 8 or 9 points; large-print editions use 16 points or more; on screens set body text around 16 pixels and judge leading by eye, since faces with a large x-height need more space between lines. Always print a test page before committing to a size.

### Keep a line of body text to about 45 to 75 characters
Valid when: the text is running prose in a single column that readers will follow from one line to the next, as in a book, article or long report, and the page or window is wide enough to hold a line of that length comfortably.
Outside that: narrow newspaper or magazine columns can drop to 35 to 45 characters; tables, code listings and lists are measured by their content rather than by this rule; in very wide layouts split the page into columns or enlarge the margins. On phones, the screen width usually sets the line length for you.

### Avoid widows and orphans at page and column breaks
Valid when: the document is a printed or paginated text, such as a book, thesis or report, where a single stranded line at the top or bottom of a page looks careless, and the layout software offers controls for keeping lines together.
Outside that: on web pages and in reflowing e-book formats the breaks change with the window size, so leave the controls on automatic; in tightly fitted layouts such as newspapers, one well-placed orphan is an acceptable compromise. Fix stubborn cases by editing a word or two rather than squeezing spacing.

### Use no more than two typefaces in one document
Valid when: the piece is an ordinary document such as a report, a menu, a leaflet or a slide deck, where one face for headings and one for body text give clear contrast, and the reader's attention should stay on the content.
Outside that: a display poster or a magazine feature may justify a third, distinctive face used sparingly for one purpose; if in doubt, create hierarchy with size, weight and spacing from a single family rather than adding faces. Check that each face you choose has proper bold and italic versions.

### Align text flush left with a ragged right edge for most documents
Valid when: the layout uses fairly narrow or medium columns, the text will be produced by people without specialist typesetting skills, and the software cannot hyphenate well, so full justification would open wide, uneven gaps between words.
Outside that: books set by experienced typesetters with good hyphenation usually look better fully justified; centred alignment suits only short items such as titles and invitations, and right alignment suits page numbers and captions. Avoid mixing several alignments on a single page.

### Practise a difficult passage slowly, at about half tempo, with a metronome
Valid when: the passage contains a technical problem, such as a fast run, an awkward shift or a tricky rhythm, that falls apart when it is played at full speed, and you can already read the notes without hesitating over them.
Outside that: once the notes are secure, raise the metronome a few clicks at a time, and also play the passage in context, starting a few bars before it; for musical shaping and phrasing, play at tempo and listen back to a recording. Stop and rest if your hands or shoulders begin to feel tense.

### Warm up for ten to fifteen minutes before a practice session
Valid when: the session involves a wind or string instrument or the voice, you will be working on demanding repertoire for half an hour or more, and you have not already played or sung earlier in the same day.
Outside that: a quick run-through before a short rehearsal can be cut to a few scales or long notes; after an injury or a long break, extend the warm-up and keep the first sessions short, gentle and free of strain. Singers may add humming, lip trills and gentle stretches of the neck and shoulders.

### Practise in several short sessions rather than one long one
Valid when: the goal is to learn new material, such as notes, fingerings or a memorised piece, attention tends to fade after twenty to thirty minutes, and your daily timetable allows you to come back to the instrument more than once.
Outside that: before a concert, also do full run-throughs at performance length to build stamina and focus; for ensemble rehearsals the schedule is set by the group, so make each of your own private sessions short and focused. Write down at the end of each session what to work on next time.

### Learn scales in all twelve keys in a fixed order around the circle of fifths
Valid when: the player is building general technique on a keyboard, string or wind instrument, has already learned a few simple keys, and wants a regular order in which each new key adds only one sharp or flat.
Outside that: for a specific exam syllabus, follow the published list of keys and patterns; for jazz or folk styles, focus on the modes and keys that the repertoire actually uses, and learn new ones as pieces call for them. Play each scale slowly and evenly before trying it fast.

### Mark breaths and bowings in pencil before the first rehearsal
Valid when: the part is new to you, it is for an orchestra, band or choir, you have had time to look through it at home, and the conductor or section leader has not yet given instructions about phrasing.
Outside that: when the leader issues an agreed set of markings, copy those exactly and rub out your own; for solo work, try several options in practice and keep marks light so they can be changed as your view of the piece develops. Never mark a borrowed library part in pen or with a highlighter.

### Record every transaction with a debit and an equal credit
Valid when: the books are kept on the double-entry system, as is normal for companies, charities and most businesses with more than a few transactions a month, whether the ledger is a paper book or an accounting program.
Outside that: a sole trader with very simple affairs may keep single-entry cash books listing money in and money out; even then, reconcile the totals to the bank statement each month so that errors are found early. Ask an accountant which system your local filing rules expect before you start.

### Reconcile the bank account every month
Valid when: the organisation has one or more bank accounts, keeps its own cash book or ledger, and receives a statement at least monthly, so the recorded balance can be compared line by line with the bank's figures.
Outside that: for very busy accounts reconcile weekly or even daily; for a dormant account a quarterly check may be enough. Unexplained differences should always be investigated rather than written off to a suspense account, and the reconciliation should be signed and dated by whoever prepared it.

### Keep receipts and invoices for at least six years
Valid when: the records support tax returns or company accounts in a jurisdiction, such as the United Kingdom, whose general retention period for business records is about six years from the end of the financial year concerned.
Outside that: check the local rules, which range from three to ten years; records for property, long-term assets and loans should be kept for as long as the asset is held, plus the normal period after it is sold. Scanned copies are usually acceptable if they are clear and kept safely backed up.

### Number invoices in one unbroken sequence
Valid when: the business issues sales invoices to customers, all of them come from one office or one accounting program, and it needs an audit trail that shows clearly that no invoice has gone missing or been deleted.
Outside that: when several branches invoice separately, give each branch its own prefix and sequence; never reuse a number for a correction — issue a credit note that refers back to the original invoice instead. Keep cancelled invoices on file, clearly marked as void, rather than throwing them away.

### Close the books on the same working day each month
Valid when: the organisation produces monthly management accounts, the people involved know the timetable in advance, and there is a regular routine of posting accruals, prepayments and reconciliations at each month end.
Outside that: a small business with simple affairs may close quarterly; at the year end allow extra time for stock counts, depreciation and the auditor's requests, and lock earlier periods once they are closed. A short written checklist helps a new member of staff follow the routine reliably.

### Cast on loosely, or with a needle one or two sizes larger
Valid when: the knitting starts with a long-tail or cable cast-on at the edge of a garment, such as a sweater hem, a hat brim or a sock cuff, which has to stretch easily over the head, hand or heel when worn.
Outside that: for a firm, non-stretch edge, such as a bag or a cushion cover, use the normal needle size; for toe-up socks and similar items, use a provisional or special cast-on that is designed for the purpose. Switch back to the pattern's needle size on the first row after casting on.

### Knit a gauge swatch at least 15 cm square before starting a garment
Valid when: the pattern gives a gauge, for example 22 stitches and 30 rows to 10 cm, you are using the yarn and needles you plan to use for the project, and the finished size matters, as it does for sweaters, hats and fitted pieces.
Outside that: scarves, blankets and shawls can usually be started without a swatch, as fit is not critical; if your count differs, change needle size rather than knitting tighter or looser by effort, and wash the swatch before measuring it. Measure in the centre, away from the edges.

### Join a new ball of yarn at the edge of the work, not in the middle
Valid when: the piece is knitted flat, has side seams or selvedges where the tails can be hidden, the yarn is a plain wool or blend, and there is enough left on the old ball to finish the row you are working.
Outside that: when knitting in the round, there is no edge, so join mid-row with a spit splice for feltable wool, or by weaving in tails on the wrong side; for slippery yarns such as silk, overlap a few stitches with both strands. Leave tails of about 15 cm so they can be woven in neatly later.

### Block finished knitting before sewing up
Valid when: the pieces are wool or another animal fibre, or a blend that will relax when wet, they need to be eased to the measurements given in the pattern, and you have a flat surface where they can be left undisturbed to dry.
Outside that: acrylic yarns respond only to gentle steaming and should not be pressed hard; textured stitches such as cables and ribbing should be pinned out lightly, without stretching, so the pattern keeps its depth. Use rust-proof pins and a towel or foam mats underneath the pieces.

### Use stitch markers every 20 to 30 stitches when casting on large numbers
Valid when: the cast-on runs to a hundred stitches or more, such as for a blanket, a shawl or the body of a sweater, you need to count accurately, and losing your place partway through would mean starting the cast-on again.
Outside that: for small items, such as a hat with 96 stitches, count in tens once and check again on the first row; in lace with repeated motifs, place a marker between each repeat so that mistakes show up within a few rows. Use a different colour of marker for the start of a round.

### In the opening, develop knights and bishops before moving the queen
Valid when: the game is in its first ten moves or so, the centre is still contested, several minor pieces remain on their starting squares, and an early queen move would let the opponent gain time by attacking it with developing moves.
Outside that: some sound openings, such as the Scandinavian Defence, bring the queen out early on purpose; when the opponent blunders material, take it if it is safe, even at the cost of slower development. Check each capture for traps before playing it, especially near your own king.

### Castle early to bring the king to safety
Valid when: the centre is open or likely to open soon, the pieces between king and rook have already been developed, and the king left on its original square would be exposed to checks along the central files.
Outside that: in closed positions with locked pawn chains, castling can wait; in some endgames and queenless middlegames the king belongs in the centre, where it is an active piece rather than a target. Before castling, check which side the opponent's pieces are aiming at.

### Fight for the centre with pawns on e4, d4, e5 or d5
Valid when: the game follows a classical opening such as the Italian Game, the Ruy Lopez or the Queen's Gambit, where central pawns gain space, open lines for the bishops and queen, and give the other pieces freedom to develop.
Outside that: hypermodern openings, such as the King's Indian or the Grünfeld, let the opponent build a pawn centre and then attack it from the flanks with pieces and pawn breaks such as c5 or f5. Choose the style that suits you and study its typical plans, not only its moves.

### Avoid moving the same piece twice in the opening
Valid when: there is no immediate tactical reason for the second move, the opponent is not threatening the piece, and the time could instead be spent developing a new piece, castling or claiming central squares.
Outside that: move a piece again to answer a direct threat, to win material, or when the opening theory itself requires it, as in some lines of the Ruy Lopez in which the bishop retreats from b5 to a4. Count how many pieces each side has developed before deciding.

### Do not grab a pawn early if it costs several moves of development
Valid when: the opponent offers a gambit, such as the King's Gambit or the Danish, you do not know the main lines well, and accepting it would leave your pieces undeveloped for several moves while the opponent attacks.
Outside that: well-studied gambits may be accepted with a known follow-up, sometimes returning the pawn later for a sound position; in rapid games, a simple and safe reply is often better than memorising long lines. Review the game afterwards to see whether the pawn was worth taking.

### Place the main subject on a rule-of-thirds line or intersection
Valid when: the photograph has a single clear subject, such as a person, a tree or a building, the background is reasonably simple, and a centred placement would look static, posed or uninteresting to the viewer.
Outside that: strongly symmetrical scenes, such as reflections, doorways and formal portraits, often work best centred; in minimal compositions a subject placed far towards one corner can give a strong sense of space. Most cameras and phones can show a grid on screen to help.

### Leave space in front of a moving or looking subject
Valid when: the subject is moving across the frame, such as a runner, a cyclist or a car, or looking to one side, as in a profile portrait or a candid street photograph, and you want the picture to feel calm and balanced.
Outside that: placing the subject near the leading edge, with the space behind, can deliberately suggest tension or leaving; for tight headshots the framing follows the face rather than any direction of gaze. Try both versions and compare them on a larger screen later.

### Keep the horizon level and away from the exact middle
Valid when: the image is a landscape or seascape with a clear horizon, the camera is held at roughly eye level, and either the sky or the foreground is clearly the more interesting part of the scene to show.
Outside that: for mirror-like reflections a centred horizon emphasises the symmetry; a deliberately tilted frame can suggest movement in sport or street photography, but should look intended rather than accidental. Small tilts can be corrected afterwards with the straighten tool in editing software.

### Use leading lines to guide the eye toward the subject
Valid when: the scene contains roads, paths, fences, rivers, railings or rows of trees that run into the frame, and you can choose a viewpoint from which they naturally point toward the main subject.
Outside that: when the lines lead out of the frame or away from the subject, change position or crop so that they do not pull attention away; in busy scenes with many lines, simplify the frame by stepping closer. A lower viewpoint often makes paths and fences more prominent.

### Check the edges of the frame before taking the picture
Valid when: the scene contains distractions near the borders, such as signposts, half-cut people, rubbish bins or bright patches, that would pull the viewer's eye out of the picture, and you have a few seconds to recompose.
Outside that: in fast-moving events with no time to compose carefully, shoot a little wider than needed and crop afterwards; in documentary work, keep the context at the edges if it tells part of the story. A small step sideways often removes a distraction completely.

### Use "fewer" with countable nouns and "less" with uncountable ones
Valid when: the text is formal written English, such as an essay, a report, a letter of application or an official notice, and the noun can clearly be counted one by one, as in "fewer mistakes" or "fewer people".
Outside that: "less" is idiomatic with distances, sums of money, time and ages, as in "less than ten miles" or "less than 30 years old"; in casual speech, "less" with plural nouns is common and widely accepted. Supermarket signs reading "ten items or less" are a familiar example.

### Place "only" next to the word it modifies
Valid when: the writing is formal, such as a contract, a set of instructions or an academic paper, and the sentence could be read in more than one way, as in "She only ate the soup" compared with "She ate only the soup".
Outside that: in everyday speech and informal writing, "only" usually sits before the verb and the context makes the meaning clear; move it only when the placement creates a real ambiguity for the reader. Reading the sentence aloud often shows whether it is clear.

### Use the Oxford comma in lists of three or more items
Valid when: the house style asks for it, as many American and academic styles do, or when leaving it out would make the list ambiguous to the reader, as in a list whose last two items could be read as describing the first.
Outside that: many British newspapers and publishers omit it as a matter of house style; whichever convention is chosen, apply it consistently throughout the document, and rewrite any sentence that remains unclear. A style sheet for the project helps several writers stay consistent.

### Use the subjunctive "were" in hypothetical "if" clauses
Valid when: the clause describes something contrary to fact or imagined, as in "If I were taller" or "If she were the manager", and the writing is formal or careful in tone, such as a speech, an essay or a published article.
Outside that: "was" is common in informal English and widely accepted; when the clause describes something that may really be true, as in "If he was there, he saw it", use the ordinary indicative "was" instead. Fixed phrases such as "as it were" always keep "were".

### Avoid splitting infinitives only when the result reads better
Valid when: moving the adverb keeps the sentence natural and clear, as in "to read carefully" in place of "to carefully read", the meaning does not change, and the writer or the publisher prefers the traditional style.
Outside that: modern usage guides accept split infinitives, and sometimes avoiding one makes the sentence awkward or changes its meaning, as in "to more than double"; keep the split when it reads most naturally. Few readers notice a split infinitive, but many notice a clumsy sentence.

### Shelve non-fiction by Dewey Decimal class number, then by author
Valid when: the library is a public or school library that uses the Dewey Decimal Classification, each book carries a spine label with its class number, and readers browse the open shelves by subject area.
Outside that: most academic and research libraries use the Library of Congress Classification instead; fiction is commonly shelved separately, in alphabetical order of author surname, with genre stickers where useful. Clear shelf-end signs help readers find the right section quickly.

### File author names surname first, then forenames
Valid when: the catalogue records modern Western names written in the usual order of forename then surname, such as "Austen, Jane" or "Dickens, Charles", and the author is known by a single, stable form of the name.
Outside that: for Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Hungarian names, the family name normally already comes first; follow the authority file, which also handles pseudonyms, titles of nobility and names with prefixes such as "de" or "van". Add cross-references from other forms of the name.

### Ignore leading articles when filing titles
Valid when: the title begins with an initial article in the language of the work, such as "The", "A" or "An" in English, or "Le" and "La" in French, and the list is sorted alphabetically for readers to browse.
Outside that: when the article is part of a name or is not an article in that language, as in "A is for Apple" or a place name like "Los Angeles", file it as written; follow the catalogue's own list of articles for each language. Most library systems apply this rule automatically.

### Assign the most specific subject heading that covers the whole work
Valid when: the item deals mainly with one topic, so a single heading such as "Roses" describes it better than a broader one such as "Gardening", and the catalogue's authority list includes a heading at that level.
Outside that: when a work covers three or more related topics evenly, use the broader heading that includes them all; for collections and anthologies add headings for form or genre as well, and check every heading against the authority list. Look at how similar books were catalogued.

### Weed the collection regularly with a simple age-and-use rule
Valid when: the library is a general lending collection with limited shelf space, the loan records are available, and items have not been borrowed for several years in a fast-changing area such as travel guides or computing.
Outside that: local history, rare items and reference works are kept regardless of use; in research libraries withdrawal follows a written policy and often involves shared regional storage instead of disposal. Worn copies of popular titles are usually replaced rather than simply removed.

### Keep coaching points to one or two per practice drill
Valid when: the players are learning or refining a skill in a drill, the session has several drills planned, and too many instructions at once would distract them from the movement they are practising.
Outside that: in a pre-match team talk, cover the few key tactical points for the game; with advanced athletes, more detailed feedback may be useful after the drill, using video review where available. Ask players to repeat the key point back in their own words.

### Plan sessions as warm-up, main part and cool-down
Valid when: the session lasts about an hour or longer, the group trains together regularly, and it includes physical activity at moderate or high effort, as in most team sports, swimming and athletics training.
Outside that: a short skills clinic for young children may simply start with active games; on match day the warm-up is set by the timing of kick-off, and the cool-down may be a short walk and stretch afterwards. Write the plan down and keep a copy of it for the next season.

### Praise effort and specific actions more than results
Valid when: the players are juniors or developing athletes whose confidence, enjoyment and long-term progress matter more than the score in any single game, and the coach sees them regularly through the season.
Outside that: with senior squads, results and performance statistics play a larger role in feedback, though specific praise for good decisions still helps; keep criticism private and constructive whenever possible. Name the action you saw, such as a well-timed pass, rather than saying "good job".

### Rotate playing positions for young players
Valid when: the players are under about twelve and still learning the game, the league is recreational rather than highly competitive, and experience in several positions helps them understand the whole team and the field.
Outside that: from the teenage years, players gradually specialise according to their strengths and interests; in competitive leagues, the coach balances squad development with the needs of each match. Explain to parents early in the season why positions are rotated.

### Let players make decisions in small-sided games
Valid when: the aim is to develop game sense and decision making, and the drill is a small-sided match, such as four against four on a reduced pitch, in which each player has many touches of the ball.
Outside that: when introducing a completely new technique, use more structured, coach-led repetition first; stop the game only for short, important teaching points, so that playing time stays high. Asking players questions during breaks helps them explain and remember their choices.

### Tip about ten to fifteen percent in restaurants where service is not included
Valid when: you are dining in a country where tipping is customary, such as the United Kingdom, the service was satisfactory, and the bill does not already include a service charge printed near the bottom of the receipt.
Outside that: in the United States, fifteen to twenty percent is normal; in Japan, tipping is generally not expected and may cause confusion; where a service charge is already on the bill, an extra tip is optional. A quick look at a recent travel guide settles most local customs.

### Dress modestly when visiting places of worship
Valid when: you are entering a church, mosque, temple, synagogue or shrine as a visitor, especially in countries where religious customs are strongly observed, and covered shoulders and knees are the usual expectation.
Outside that: some sites lend scarves or wraps at the door, and some ask visitors to remove shoes as well; check the posted rules, and ask before taking photographs, particularly during services or ceremonies. Carrying a light scarf in a day bag makes unplanned visits easy.

### Learn a few polite phrases in the local language
Valid when: you are travelling in a country whose main language you do not speak, and you will meet shopkeepers, hosts, drivers and waiting staff in everyday situations, such as greetings, ordering food and saying thank you.
Outside that: in business settings, use the language agreed for the meeting and bring an interpreter if needed; where people are clearly comfortable in your language, follow their lead rather than insisting on your own attempts. A small phrasebook or offline translation app is useful backup.

### Stand on one side of the escalator and leave the other free
Valid when: the city has a clear local custom, such as standing on the right in London, the escalator is wide enough for two people side by side, and people in a hurry can walk past on the other side.
Outside that: some cities stand on the left, and some transport operators now ask everyone to stand on both sides at busy times; watch what local people do and follow any signs or announcements. Keep bags and children close to you so the walking side stays clear.
